Ken Arnold, who wrote the curses library and worked on Rogue with Michael Toy at Berkeley,
talks to the Northeastern University Game Development Club in Boston: how Toy came looking for
him with curses questions, Rogue's spread on the Berkeley Unix tapes and the ARPANET, the rules
behind its random dungeons, starvation and fixed starting stats as answers to players who gamed
the system, securing the score and save files (and how Ken Thompson got round it), why people
played it for days on end, and the roguelikes that followed. In the questions he talks about
Rog-O-Matic, the one game he won, and how the source of Rogue 5.3, which he demonstrates, survived
only because someone stole a copy of a backup tape.

See also the [2016 panel with Glenn Wichman and Michael Toy](rogue-panel-2016.md).

## From Santa Cruz to Berkeley

[**2:02**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=122s) So originally Michael and Glenn Wichman wrote this when they were at UC Santa Cruz, hanging out in the late — very late — 70s. It was very quickly very popular on the Unix systems of the time. Now, what was going on there at the time was the ARPANET, which is the precursor to the internet. The ARPANET was being developed, and Berkeley was being paid to develop BSD Unix as the primary new operating system for the internet. There were some other operating systems that already existed that were being plugged in, but the idea was that Unix could be made into a very good network operating system, or operating system for the network.

[**2:51**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=171s) So all the Unix distributions that weren't coming from Bell Labs were coming from Berkeley. They were sending out computer mag tapes, and there were probably about 60 or 70 institutions that directly got those tapes, and then they made copies and sent out tapes. So when we say that this was popular on Unix systems, there were probably on the order of 1,500 to 2,000 computers at the time, and each computer was shared among multiple people, so you might say a computer had an average of between 10 and 30 users.

[**3:33**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=213s) So Mike transferred to Berkeley in 1980, and he went looking for me to ask curses questions. (I'll show you Rogue in a minute, but it's built on top of a library I wrote called curses, which was designed for character-matrix terminals. It'll be easier to describe it when I point at it, and I'll do it fast; anyway, you probably know.) So he was using this library that I wrote, so he came looking for me; and I had been playing this for a while, and I had my frustrations with the game, so I went looking for him. So we sort of ran smack into each other, and then teamed up, and we did the primary development from Rogue 2.0 to 5.3, the last we put out. And then Jon Lane ported it to the IBM PC. This was well before Windows.

## A game its author could enjoy

[**4:25**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=265s) The main thing that Michael was trying to do when he started out was to write a game that he personally could enjoy playing after having written it. The reason this was particularly interesting to him is that the primary games at the time were Adventure and Zork and games of that ilk, which were essentially mazes. You run into the wizard, and there's a magical set of things you have to do to get past the wizard, and then you would come to the gulch where the dragon would attack. But they were the same, right? If you knew what to do, you could write a program that would just send the right text to the terminal at the right time, and you'd be done. And of course, once you've written one of those things, there was no point in playing it; you knew all the answers. And his idea, which shockingly was new at the time, was he wanted to write a game where he could have adventures but be able to play it himself, even though he'd written it. We used to call these rat-in-a-maze games, and frankly they bored the crap out of me too, so we were well matched.

[**5:27**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=327s) So the idea is you have random generation with the basic rules, and you regenerate the universe each time, but the metaverse is always the same. There's a fundamental set of rules that govern play, govern the kinds of items that can be found, how they interact with each other. So the question is not "What are the rules of this universe?" — after you get playing, you learn the rules that apply to each universe — but "How do I apply the knowledge I have gained previously to this new world that I have in front of me, that I haven't seen before?"

[**6:00**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=360s) It was really important not to reward mindless play. If people played Rogue, they would literally sit at the terminal and play for hours — I know this doesn't shock you at all — and this was also pretty new at the time. So what we didn't want to reward is, for example — there was a strategy where you could drop a scroll of scare monster and stand on it, so the monsters wouldn't hit you, and they'd gather around you, and you put your book on the space bar, and then you go get lunch. And then you come back, and by then you have a million monsters gathered around you, and you've fainted, but then you eat some food, so now you can fight again, and then you have a million monsters to kill off, and lo and behold, a lot of experience. That's when we introduced dying of hunger. Because we don't want to reward that kind of — we want people to be playing the game, not outsmarting it with clever little tricks they can play to get around the game, and people [were] doing that all the time. And as I said before, it should be hard to win. In fact, when you look at roguelike games, this is one of the really defining characteristics, I think: it's going to be hard to win, and we are going to keep screwing with you forever to make it happen.

[**7:17**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=437s) So it's a turn-based game: all combat, everything, is turn-based. It's done on a terminal, and since a lot of you have played it, you know what it looks like. I'll show it in a second, for those of you who don't. You look down on the grid of rooms. The graphics are limited to printable characters — at that point, that was all you could rely upon. The window size: 24 lines, 80 characters, dead-ass standard. You are the @, monsters are A through Z, scrolls are question marks, rings are equals signs, armor is a bracket, and so on; there's a set of other symbolic characters that represent things you pick up in the dungeon. And it's a permadeath game.

[**7:58**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=478s) And this is where — when you look at the roguelike games, there's controversy still over whether or not you are a roguelike game if you don't have permadeath. But it was important in the way we played it: you play and you play and you play, and then you screw up, and you just have to start over.

## The rules of the universe

[**10:44**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=644s) So this is the metaverse, the rules of the underlying universe generation system. At the highest scale, each level has rooms, doors (which can be hidden, and you have to search for them), hallways and a staircase down. What's random is where exactly the rooms are placed within the grid, which rooms connect to which, which rooms are lit and which rooms are dark. On level three there's a certain set of monsters that you can potentially encounter, but their placement and their generation — wandering monsters that show up during play — is random.

[**11:23**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=683s) There's a fixed set of magical items: there's a potion of speed, and there's a scroll of teleportation, and so on. They're labeled randomly each time you start the game, so when you pick something up the first time, you don't know what it is. You can discover it many ways: there's a scroll of identify; you can use it and see what it does — that's sometimes smart and sometimes very stupid, but in desperation you'll do anything. There are all sorts of ways to try and figure it out. Once you do that, the game remembers what label is on the scroll and will tell you "That's a scroll of identify" the next time you pick one up. But when you first get into the dungeon, you're a neophyte; every time you start, you're a neophyte, and you don't know what a scroll of identify actually looks like.

[**12:04**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=724s) Sort of fun playing around with colors, because it's all words. For example, I used plaid as a color for the potion: a plaid potion. And again, armor and weapons are of known types, and what you find is random. And your starting stats are always the same. We initially did randomize people's starting stats, and — this is the "do not reward people who want to play in a boring way" — they just started rerolling over and over and over until they got good stats, and then proceeded. So we said no, can't do that.

## Popularity and cheating

[**12:44**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=764s) So when they were sending out BSD Unix, it was on the tape, and it went to everybody who had an ARPANET node. When we spoke at USENIX in '82, which was in Boston, Mike and I gave a talk on Rogue, and it was the most highly attended talk. USENIX is where the Unix people gathered and talked, and Rogue filled the seats. It's hard to talk about stats and how popular things were, but —

[**13:14**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=794s) This kind of behavior was pretty new. This kind of behavior, of course, we all do, but this was the first time anybody ever really did this with a computer game, so we were stumped — absolutely jaw-dropped. People would talk to us about not doing work for three days on end while they played Rogue, and missing dates, those people who had girlfriends. And people would get together and do parallel play: they'd all sit in the same room and play Rogue. Since it's a single-player game, that means they're all just ignoring each other in parallel. And that was a new experience; [I'd] never seen anything else do that.

[**14:00**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=840s) And of course, popularity led to cheating. There's a shared score file on each computer. Since the computers were shared, that meant — not everybody in your network, because the network software was pretty new then, but everybody who shared that machine with you would share a score file with you. And that meant, to us — Mike and I felt that we had some obligation to at least discourage people from screwing around with the score file and getting scores in ways that were not playing straight. Somebody who doesn't know how to hack a computer ought to be able to get the high score, right? That seems totally different. So we had to secure both the score file and the save file. We did a lot of checks on the save file: when we saved the file to disk, we would know the inode number (its identity on the disk), and the save timestamp, and all sorts of things, to try and make sure that nobody had played with it.

[**14:55**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=895s) At one point we got to notice that Ken Thompson, who was one of the inventors of Unix, used to play with a disk: he would mount it, and then he would raw-copy the disk so that he could start it over and over and over again. Okay, I can't stop that, but most people don't have that access.

## Why people played

[**15:10**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=910s) So to us this was a really shocking thing. Why is this — what about this is popular? Why are people playing this instead of, I don't know — there was a solitaire game, there was Hunt the Wumpus, there was a 20 Questions game, there was Adventure, which people would play a lot until they solved it. So it was popular partly because these kinds of cursor-addressable terminals, where you could draw a picture on the window like that and then go back to a point and change it — that was brand spanking new. That's how long ago this was.

[**15:51**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=951s) So partly it was using the latest graphics system. There were much better graphics systems, but they cost way too much money for most people to put their hands on. Starting play was very simple, so it was easy to get up and start down this path, but it was impossible to win, and that was, I think, important to its popularity. You get to the point where you absolutely frustrate the ever-loving crap out of people who are trying to play the game, and they are goddamn going to beat it next time; they keep coming back, going to beat it. So this was surprising to me, but the fact that it was so difficult to win was partly what kept people coming back. It was as immersive as was possible, again, for the time, because that's what graphics systems were. But it was direct, interactive: it didn't print out a bunch of text that you had to read and then, in your mind, create the picture. The picture was right in front of you.

[**16:39**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=999s) Which led us to this brilliant understanding — and you all probably have that anyway — but immersivity is in your head. What constitutes being inside a space happens between the ears, not on the screen. It's a matter of expectations. [I remember] seeing a psychological experiment where they put a screen in front of your eyes, and then they touch a dummy while they're touching you, and your mind maps the two of them together. And so then when they hit the dummy in the stomach, you flinch, because you think you're about to be hit. It's all up here. It's a matter of what people expect to see; the level of graphics is all a matter of expectations.

[**17:18**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1038s) To us, in some sense, this felt like how slot machines are addictive. That is, it's easy to put the first quarter in, and we give you a lot of little wins, because you beat this monster, and you beat that monster, and you figured out how to get through this doorway, and figured out the maze of this particular level. But you never get to the big win, so [you] just keep going; the big win is just over the hill.

[**17:42**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1062s) And also there's constant change and motion. I don't know about you, but it's a fairly standard human experience: you may sit on the beach and watch the waves roll in, or look at a fire, and find that it's not very exciting, but nonetheless it's rather calming and attractive; most people [can] sit and do that for a long time. There's a certain rate of change that's seductive. And on the screen, as you saw, you move around, the room pops up, you go down a level, there's a whole new level, and yet it persists long enough, because you have to look around the room. The same is true, again, of slot machines: you pull the lever and it rolls, and it goes dink, dink, and there's a pause, and dink, dink, when you roll it over again. So there's this rate of change that isn't too fast but isn't too slow that I think pulls people along.

## The games that followed

[**18:43**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1123s) So it spawned — much to our original — you have no idea how weird this was to us. Nobody had played a game in this kind of way before, and people were asking us how it worked, why it was popular, and we didn't know the answers to this. We were actually programmers; we weren't even game designers — there was no such field. We weren't in the psych department. We had no idea why this was happening, and people were piling in and trying to pick this thing apart. And one of the things that surprised us a great deal was that people started writing their own games that were in the same style, in the category, as you all know, [that] still exist.

[**19:34**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1174s) NetHack is the closest one to the original Rogue design. It has some graphic tiles, for people who like graphic tiles, but primarily it's again a character-matrix-based game. ## What makes a roguelike

[**22:45**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1365s) So the real — I thought I had a slide in here, but I haven't come to it, so I think I may have lost it — is the section on what's the core constituency of what a roguelike game is. A lot of it is this: the random generation is critical, but a lot of it is difficulty of winning, and how much grief you get for losing. So the permadeath question is something people argue about forever: is that a good feature or a bad thing? Me, I think it's critical. I think it really makes a difference if you feel like everything's on the line when you make an attack. But other people, of course, like to be able to — they don't like to go backwards.

[**23:26**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1406s) So this struck me many years ago, which is that most of the first-person shooters and the RPGs that [people] play are all rat-in-a-maze things: once you've done it, you're done. And considering that Rogue came in early and was very popular, I was surprised that there weren't more games that took the lesson about randomization to heart. So once you play the game, you're done. You might replay it on hard or killer, or try and see if you can beat your old time, but at some point you're just [treading] the same ground. One of the reasons I was thinking this might be true is that, of course, once you're done with it, you have to buy another game, so this may be why people don't think it's a good thing. But the Mystery Dungeon games are very popular, and they sell, so I don't know whether that's it or not.

[**24:22**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1462s) And then the sandbox games, like Minecraft, famous at the moment: they have randomization, but they don't have any of these other attributes of play. But at least you walk into a world, and it's different each time, even though it's based on the same set of rules. So it was surprising to me at the time that Rogue became popular, and it's surprising to me, considering it was popular, how little it was followed. So both ways, whatever.

## Lessons

[**24:58**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1498s) And so I know the lessons we pulled out of this — I pulled out of this, and this is not my field, so I want to hear from you people who have been exposed to this. The ones that are easy for me to pull out — [unclear] — excuse me, I seem to have lost the last slide there. So, first of all, graphics obviously can be very simple. As long as people can recognize themselves and place themselves in the game, they're okay. Another is that people are driven to small, consecutive building goals, and [you] really can turn their frustration into addiction. That's really what it boils down to, at least as we saw it: once you have become sufficiently frustrated, you are going to get it next time. You say to yourself, "I am going to beat it next time. I was close, and I'll do better," and people will iterate over and over and over again.

[**26:01**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1561s) The other main thing — it's been interesting to me to see. FTL is new, and even though Diablo seems to have pulled back from it, there [are] other games. There seems to be at the moment — again, from my unprofessional and unimmersive opinion — some bubbling up of this style of game again; people are trying to make it. You look at, say, FTL: it came out of Kickstarter, and what that means is they don't have to please anybody else except the people who want it right then. They don't have this planned-obsolescence intrinsic benefit that the other companies get: if they do a planned-obsolescence system, the game is boring, you buy a new one. But all FTL has to do is make the people who want to buy it right now happy; that's their entire goal. And as you know, Kickstarter and other equivalent things make it easier and easier to fund things in that kind of way, that go direct to the consumer and please the consumer, rather than please some general business model that you can turn into a business plan that you can sell on the stock market. I think more of these are bubbling up. So I don't have any major, huge things out of this, but in the sense of — again, this wasn't my profession, to analyze this — there are some lessons I've brought from it. I'm interested in hearing from you people who have played them and liked them, any differences you might have with what I said about it. And I continue to be surprised that people who were born long after these games were popular are still interested in this and still pushing, so that makes me happy to be here.

## Questions: commands and auto-explore

[**27:48**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1668s) Any questions, comments? — Yes. Can you speak at all about whether one of the [reasons] that you had 20 people congregating for your next session was the complexity inherent in the game, compared to many current games — like the keyboard interface being completely new at the time, and still not being used since — or anything else other than —

[**28:05**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jIOwtgQKs7k&t=1685s) Well, I mentioned curses earlier.
